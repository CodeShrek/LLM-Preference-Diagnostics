Reviewer: 1
Comments:
The authors propose a behavioral framework that separates whether an LLM commits to a directional action from
which action it selects given commitment.
Strengths.
- The question is relevant: forced-choice designs can indeed conflate commitment with direction, and offering an
explicit non-directional option is a sensible design choice.
- The factorial design, with payoff ratio and presentation order varied independently, is a reasonable way to separate
sign, magnitude and order effects.
Major concerns.
1. The experimental task is not described, so the findings cannot be evaluated or reproduced. This is my main
concern. The manuscript never shows or adequately defines the task on which every result depends:
  - What is the "simulated environment," and what do the Add and Subtract operations act on? The text says only that
they "have effects that carry quality payoff rewards in some quantitative form."
  - What happens in the observation and exploration turns? What information does the model receive, are these turns
scripted, and do the model's earlier replies affect later turns?
Without this information, a reader cannot tell whether the headline behaviors reflect properties of the models or of this
particular prompt.
2. The conclusions are broader than the evidence supports.
  - The study uses one task template, four small (3-4B) 4-bit-quantized models, and a single deterministic response
per condition.
  - Variation across phrasings is not reported at all.
  - The title and framing refer to "LLM preference elicitation" in general, but the results cannot separate effects of
model size, architecture, training or quantization
At minimum:
  - report results per phrasing variant;
  - include larger and non-quantized models;
  - test at least one structurally different task.
Presentation. The manuscript requires minor editing before it can be assessed as a journal article. Examples:
- Template leftovers: the running header "Author et al.:", the LaTeX artifact "justification=centering" printed under Fig.
1
Recommendation. The question is worthwhile, but in its current form the manuscript does not describe its task well
enough to be evaluated or reproduced. Its conclusions also extend beyond the narrow evidence. I therefore
recommend major revision. A substantially revamped experiment study that releases all materials, reports descriptive
results, uses appropriate methods for separation, and covers a broader set of models and tasks could make a useful
contribution.
Additional Questions:
1. Which category describes this manuscript?: Research
2. How relevant is this manuscript to the readers? Explain under Public Comments.: Relevant
1. Please explain how this manuscript advances the field of research and/or contributes something new to the
literature.: The manuscript argues that forced-choice preference tests for LLMs mix two behaviors:
- whether a model commits to a directional action at all;
- which action it selects once it commits.
It proposes separating the two with an explicit "indifferent" option and a two-stage analysis. The authors evaluate four
small (3-4B), quantized, instruction-tuned models on a three-turn task.
The idea is sensible and relevant to LLM evaluation. More importantly, it cannot be verified: the task, prompts,
dilemmas and payoff values are not described, and no materials are released. The findings are also limited to one
task and four small models, so they do not support general conclusions about LLM preference elicitation.

Interesting measurement problem, and relevant to OJCS readers working on LLM eval and agent behavior.
Separating commitment from conditional choice is useful. Balanced payoff/order design, explicit trial accounting, and
restraint about latent preferences are positives. Qwen's 955/1120 indifferent responses alongside perfect C4
performance is especially worth following up. But I'd need substantial methods detail and reanalysis before accepting
current conclusions.
1. Task needs to be reproable. Please supply all 20 dilemmas, four phrasing templates, exact system/user prompts for
each turn, action semantics, exploration/state-update rules, starting scores and thresholds, and definition of success.
Fig 1 shows both Add and Subtract increasing score, names alone don't explain what these operations mean. Also
explain whether exploration is scripted or model-generated. If earlier outputs differ by model, final-turn comparison
includes different trajectories. Include example full transcripts plus prompt generator, raw outputs, parser and analysis
code in a review-accessible artifact. 'Available on reasonable request' leaves too much of this methods paper
unverifiable.
2. Statistical specification needs improvement. Please write out multinomial, Stage 1, full Stage 2 and simplified Stage
2 models, including all fixed effects and exact order coding. Dilemma fixed effects don't by themselves account for
residual dependence among variants of a dilemma. Stage 1 also has very few non-directional outcomes for
Llama/Phi, only 24 and 14 across 20 dilemmas. Report separation/convergence diagnostics there too. Prefer a
separation-robust fit, eg bias-reduced or suitably regularized logistic analysis, with dilemma-aware uncertainty.
Describe bootstrap CI construction, seed, convergence criteria, failed/separated replicates and how they were
handled. 10k resamples alone doesn't establish valid uncertainty with only 20 clusters.

coefficients match full-MLE interactions in text, no adjustment for those interactions is described. Fig 7 also profiles
models using separation-sensitive coefficients. Refit or label these exploratory, and add uncertainty rather than using

Reviewer: 3
Comments:
The manuscript addresses an important methodological issue in LLM preference elicitation by separating directional
commitment from payoff sensitivity conditional on commitment. The factorial design and control analyses are valuable.
However, several concerns require substantial revision.
Major Comments:
Construct Validity: The indifferent option yields zero payoff, while both directional actions appear to provide positive
payoffs, including at parity. This raises questions about whether Stage 1 measures directional commitment or
selection of a positive-payoff action over a zero-payoff default. Qwen's 85.3% indifferent rate despite 100% C4
accuracy also deserves further explanation.
Statistical Reliability: Stage 2 suffers from quasi-complete separation. Qwen's full-MLE coefficient (3.319) and the
reported stabilized interval [0.84, 2.16] come from different model specifications. The authors should report
corresponding estimates and intervals together and consider separation-resistant methods, such as Firth's penalized
likelihood.
Payoff and Threshold Design: The absolute payoff values, starting scores, and threshold conditions should be
clarified. For example, a starting score of 4 with Q = 9 would cross a threshold of 10, apparently contradicting the
description of the follow-up threshold as non-binding.
Cross-Model Comparisons: Stage 2 includes 161 committed non-parity trials for Qwen compared with 960 for
Gemma. Such differences introduce selection concerns that limit direct cross-model comparisons. The authors should
address these limitations or qualify their conclusions.
Numerical-Comprehension Control: The C4 protocol and differing sample sizes require clarification. The claimed
consistency between C4 accuracy and Stage 2 coefficient rankings is also inaccurate because Llama and Phi reverse
positions. This interpretation should be corrected.
Reproducibility and Reporting: The authors should provide the experimental prompts, dilemma descriptions,
response-classification rules, and analysis scripts. Table 4's definitions of valid and invalid trials should also be
reconciled with the reported results.
Minor Comments:
1. The "Subtract" label appears to increase the score, potentially introducing semantic bias.
2. Sample Qwen transcripts would help explain the frequent indifferent responses.
3. Descriptions suggesting that the evaluated models were locally trained should be corrected.
4. Language editing and removal of formatting artifacts would improve presentation.
Overall Assessment:
The study offers a relevant methodological contribution, but concerns regarding construct validity, statistical reliability,
and experimental reproducibility should be addressed before publication. Therefore, I recommend Revise and
Resubmit, as substantial revisions are needed to strengthen the study and support its conclusions.
