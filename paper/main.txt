\IEEEtitleabstractindextext{%
\begin{abstract}Standard language-model preference evaluations use forced-choice protocols compelling selection between directional actions, conflating whether an agent commits to action with which option it selects. We propose a behavioral diagnostic separating directional commitment from conditional payoff sensitivity via an explicit \textit{Indifferent} option. Across 4,480 trials spanning four small model families (Qwen 2.5 3B, Llama 3.2 3B, Phi-4 Mini 3.8B, Gemma 3 4B) and 20 dilemmas, forced choice obscures fundamental behavioral divergences. When permitted to decline action, directive models (Llama, Phi, Gemma) commit in $\ge 97.8\%$ of trials, whereas Qwen 2.5 3B abstains on 85.3\%. Conditional on committing, Qwen displays positive payoff sensitivity ($\hat\beta=1.231$, 95\% CI $[0.50,1.96]$); however, dominant-choice accuracy drops to 49.0\% in the disfavored slot under default labels. Counterbalancing ($N=1,120$) reveals that Qwen's abstention is moderated by action names and option markers (disparity commitment surging from 16.8\% to 87.6\% with 98.7\% accuracy), while at parity, selections favored Option~1 on the 34.4\% of trials where the model committed. Llama and Phi show moderate sensitivity ($\hat\beta=0.282, 0.225$), while Gemma achieves near-ceiling dominant accuracy (99.5\%). Matched forced choice compels Qwen into an extreme 79:21 positional skew at parity, showing that forced choice can manufacture directional bias.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) as autonomous decision-making agents has accelerated empirical efforts to characterize model behavior in terms of values, utility functions, preferences, and alignment with human objectives. In standard evaluation benchmarks, a common paradigm presents an agent with multiple alternatives and infers a latent directional preference from the selected option. Such protocols are methodologically attractive because they convert complex evaluative questions into discrete, quantifiable choices. Yet in measurement theory, the mapping from an observed categorical output to an underlying behavioral construct is not automatic: a directional response can arise because the model has a robust preference, because the prompt induces a positional or linguistic heuristic, because the model resolves an unstated ambiguity, or simply because the protocol compels a forced choice even when alternatives are mathematically indistinguishable.

This gap creates a fundamental construct-validity problem for preference elicitation. In a conventional two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) design, an agent must select one of two alternatives. Consequently, the protocol cannot separate a directional commitment from an outcome that would have been expressed as indifference, abstention, or neutrality had such an option been available. While decision-theoretic economics has long recognized that offering a no-choice or deferral option fundamentally alters revealed choice under conflict \cite{dhar1997consumer,tversky1992choice}, LLM evaluations remain overwhelmingly anchored in forced-choice prompts. Recent empirical studies show that language-model choices can exhibit severe measurement instability under procedural variation \cite{randomness_not_rep_2025}, showing substantial sensitivity to presentation order, prompt framing, and token formatting \cite{fragile_preferences_2026,innate_econ_preferences_2026,zheng2023large}. Furthermore, elicitation protocols materially alter the gap between stated and behaviorally revealed choices when models are allowed to abstain \cite{mind_the_gap_2026}. Taken together, these findings motivate an evaluation decomposition that asks not only \emph{which} alternative is selected, but also \emph{whether the model commits directionally at all}.

The present study addresses this challenge by developing and evaluating a two-stage behavioral diagnostic framework within a consequential, multi-turn task environment. The task provides the model with two data-processing operations, referred to as \textit{Add} and \textit{Subtract}, whose execution produces verifiable state updates carrying quantitatively specified quality payoffs. An explicit \textit{Indifferent} response is always available alongside the directional options at the initial decision turn. By systematically varying payoff ratios from 1:9 through 9:1 across 20 structural dilemmas and counterbalancing presentation position, our framework separates signed preference direction from payoff-magnitude sensitivity. Selecting a directional action triggers a scripted downstream state update (Turn~2) and a follow-on decision (Turn~3), embedding choice within an operational task pipeline rather than an abstract survey.

Crucially, our inquiry focuses strictly on \emph{observable elicited behavior}. We do not claim to identify unobservable internal utility functions, prove psychological indifference, or attribute human-like beliefs or desires to language models. Rather, we investigate five concrete research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1:}] \textit{Commitment Propensity:} Do instruction-tuned models commit directionally to action when an explicit non-directional alternative is costlessly available, and does commitment scale with payoff disparity?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2:}] \textit{Conditional Payoff Sensitivity:} Conditional on committing, do models select the task-defined mathematically dominant alternative, and how sensitive is this conditional choice to payoff disparity?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3:}] \textit{Presentation Moderation:} To what extent are apparent commitment and directional preferences moderated or confounded by presentation position and action labeling?
    \item[\textbf{RQ4:}] \textit{Mechanism Disentanglement:} Can observed behavioral divergences (such as high abstention alongside high arithmetic competence) be explained by numerical comparison limitations, threshold-crossing goals, or heuristic decision rules?
    \item[\textbf{RQ5:}] \textit{Protocol Distortions:} What observable distortions emerge when the non-directional exit is eliminated in a matched two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) design?
\end{enumerate}

We evaluate this protocol across four locally deployed instruction-tuned models in the 3--4B parameter tier (Qwen 2.5 3B, Llama 3.2 3B, Phi-4 Mini 3.8B, and Gemma 3 4B) under deterministic greedy decoding ($T=0.0$). This paper makes three primary contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A Two-Stage Hurdle Elicitation Protocol:} We introduce a diagnostic framework that formally decouples directional commitment from conditional payoff discrimination in multi-turn environments, avoiding the confounding inherent in single-stage forced choice.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Heterogeneity and Heuristic Confounding Across Model Families:} Across 4,480 primary factorial trials, we show that baseline commitment propensity diverges radically across architectures (Qwen abstains on 85.3\% of trials, whereas Llama, Phi, and Gemma commit on $\ge 97.8\%$). Conditional on commitment, apparent payoff sensitivity is heavily moderated by presentation position for Qwen and Phi, whereas Gemma exhibits near-ceiling presentation invariance.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation of Forced-Choice Artifacts and Factorial Counterbalancing:} Through a matched 1,120-trial 2AFC baseline on Qwen, we show that forced choice manufactures an extreme 79:21 directional skew at parity where the model is otherwise 97.5\% indifferent. Furthermore, an exhaustive full-factorial neutral counterbalancing experiment ($N=1,120$) reveals that Qwen's headline abstention default surges to 87.6\% commitment with 98.7\% dominant accuracy and balanced action choices (50.9\% Alpha / 49.1\% Beta, reflecting balanced task dominance paired with 98.7\% dominant accuracy), indicating that apparent commitment defaults are co-determined by action names and option markers.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Elicitation Protocols and Abstention}
Standard preference elicitation in LLMs often relies on forced-choice prompting between two alternatives. While convenient, forced-choice formats can mask model uncertainty, ambiguity, or indifference. Mahajan et al.~\cite{mind_the_gap_2026} showed that elicitation protocol materially alters the gap between stated and behaviorally revealed preferences, showing that allowing models to abstain fundamentally changes observable choice distributions. Notably, in subjective conversational risk dilemmas, Mahajan et al. reported that Gemma 3 4B chose ``Equal Preference'' in approximately 70\% of revealed cases. As we show, however, in a quantitative procedural pipeline, Gemma exhibits an opposite, strictly directive default (0.0\% abstention across all 1,120 primary trials), highlighting that commitment defaults interact heavily with task framing. Wen et al.~\cite{wen_abstention_2025} provide a comprehensive survey of abstention mechanisms in LLMs, noting that instruction-tuned models often lack well-calibrated abstention behaviors unless explicitly prompted. In utility elicitation, Mazeika et al.~\cite{mazeika2025utility} examined structural coherence in elicited utility representations under forced-choice pairwise designs. Complementing this, Khan et al.~\cite{randomness_not_rep_2025} showed that multiple-choice evaluation formats can exhibit high sensitivity to arbitrary prompt framing and option order, cautioning that measured alignment often reflects superficial evaluation artifacts rather than robust latent traits.

\textit{Novelty Relative to Prior Abstention and Elicitation Frameworks:} Our framework makes three distinct methodological contributions relative to prior LLM abstention benchmarks. First, while decision-theoretic economics has long established that offering a no-choice or deferral option fundamentally alters revealed choice under conflict \cite{dhar1997consumer,tversky1992choice}, LLM preference elicitation has remained overwhelmingly anchored in two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) pairwise prompts. Second, unlike prior abstention studies that focus on epistemic uncertainty (``I don't know''), guardrail refusals, or conversational query answering \cite{wen_abstention_2025,mind_the_gap_2026}, our diagnostic evaluates consequential choices in an operational task where actions execute verifiable data transformations carrying quantitative quality payoffs. Third, whereas prior work treats abstention as an aggregate outcome, we formulate a two-part hurdle protocol that formally decouples directional commitment from conditional payoff discrimination. This enables us to show empirically that forced-choice protocols manufacture artificial directional imbalances at parity and conflate an agent's commitment threshold with its conditional payoff discrimination.

\subsection{Position and Order Effects}
A recognized challenge in LLM evaluation is sensitivity to the order, format, and surface tokens of presented alternatives \cite{pezeshkpour2023large,sclar2024quantifying,zhao2021calibrate}. Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2023large} showed that LLMs are vulnerable to multiple-choice selection bias, exhibiting substantial prior preferences toward specific option identifiers (such as Option~A vs.~Option~B) independent of content. Yin et al.~\cite{fragile_preferences_2026} systematically investigated order effects in preference judgments, showing that positional manipulations can alter observed choices even when underlying options remain unchanged. Buchanan and Foster~\cite{innate_econ_preferences_2026} and Ross et al.~\cite{ross2024llmeconomicus} documented similar fragility in economic and decision-theoretic choice tasks. These findings motivate our factorial design, where presentation position is effect-coded and crossed independently with payoff dominance to test both main effects and position-by-payoff interactions.

\subsection{Behavioral and Consequential Evaluation}
Recent work has examined LLM preferences through consequential allocation games, revealed-preference axioms, and sequential decision environments \cite{chen2023emergent,what_do_llms_want_2026,probing_preferences_2025,ai_revealed_preferences_2026}. Cook et al.~\cite{what_do_llms_want_2026} evaluated economic preferences across stylized distribution tasks, while Wang et al.~\cite{ai_revealed_preferences_2026} evaluated revealed task preferences through forced-choice experiments requiring models to perform chosen tasks. These approaches share the premise that preference evaluation should rely on consequential choices within a task rather than abstract self-reports. Our framework embeds choice in a multi-turn transformation task where actions produce verifiable downstream consequences on data state within a simulated execution environment.

\subsection{Reliability and Construct Validity}
The broader LLM-evaluation literature increasingly emphasizes construct validity and measurement reliability when interpreting empirical behaviors \cite{randomness_not_rep_2025}, warning against treating surface-level survey choices as stable human-like attitudes or demographic reflections \cite{whos_opinions_lm_2023,aher2023turing}. A common measurement failure is inferring stable latent preferences from outputs that actually reflect prompt artifacts, position heuristics, or arithmetic limitations. In keeping with this construct-validity perspective, our estimands describe observable probabilities of response categories under specified factorial manipulations, without attributing latent psychological utility or human-like desires to the models. Furthermore, to control for precision scaling artifacts without conflating parameter scale \cite{dettmers2023case}, our primary evaluation focuses strictly on the 3--4B instruction-tuned class, with higher-precision evaluation scripts released for open benchmarking.

\section{Measurement Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Operational Constructs}
The outcome space contains three mutually exclusive response categories at the decision turn:
\textit{Add}, \textit{Subtract}, and \textit{Indifferent}. The first two are directional actions; the third is an explicitly permitted non-directional response. For analysis, a trial is classified as a \textit{commitment} when the output is either \textit{Add} or \textit{Subtract}; it is classified as \textit{non-directional} when the output is \textit{Indifferent}. This coding is deliberately behavioral: it does not distinguish whether an indifference response arises from equal valuation, uncertainty, caution, refusal, or procedural framing, as these latent causes are not identifiable from categorical output alone.

Conditional on commitment, each non-parity trial has a mathematically dominant option: the option associated with the larger quality payoff. A committed response is therefore coded as either \textit{Dominant} or \textit{Dominated} relative to the task-defined payoff relation. This provides the second stage of the analysis without requiring a latent utility interpretation.

\subsection{Payoff Representation}
To evaluate payoff sensitivity, we define the signed payoff disparity ratio
\begin{equation}
    r = \frac{Q_{\mathrm{add}}}{Q_{\mathrm{sub}}}.
\end{equation}

The signed contrast $\log r$ is used in the joint multinomial formulation. Positive values indicate that \textit{Add} is mathematically dominant, negative values indicate that \textit{Subtract} is dominant, and zero corresponds to parity. Because the ratios are reciprocally paired (1:9, 1:5, 1:2, 1:1, 2:1, 5:1, 9:1), $\log r$ provides a symmetric scale for directional comparisons.

For analyses in which the identity of the dominant option should not affect the predictor, we use the magnitude
\begin{equation}
    m = \frac{\max(Q_{\mathrm{add}},Q_{\mathrm{sub}})}{\min(Q_{\mathrm{add}},Q_{\mathrm{sub}})},
\end{equation}
with $\log(m)=|\log r|$. Thus, $|\log r|$ is zero at parity and increases symmetrically as the payoff departs from 1:1. This allows the Stage 1 commitment analysis to test whether stronger payoff disparity increases the probability of acting, independent of which option is dominant. Across all primary experimental dilemmas, absolute payoff values are fixed with the baseline minimum set to $Q_{\mathrm{min}} = \min(Q_{\mathrm{add}}, Q_{\mathrm{sub}}) = 1$, such that the dominant payoff equals the disparity ratio: $Q_{\mathrm{max}} = \max(Q_{\mathrm{add}}, Q_{\mathrm{sub}}) = m \in \{1, 2, 5, 9\}$ points per entry (e.g., at a 1:5 ratio, $Q_{\mathrm{sub}} = 5$ and $Q_{\mathrm{add}} = 1$).

\subsection{Joint Multinomial Formulation}
The full three-category outcome can be formulated as a joint multinomial logit model. For directional action $k\in\{\text{Add},\text{Subtract}\}$ relative to the non-directional reference category (\textit{Indifferent}), the model is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:multinomial}
\begin{aligned}
\log\left(\frac{P(Y_i=k)}{P(Y_i=\text{Indifferent})}\right)
={}& \beta_{0k}+\beta_{1k}\log(r_i)+\beta_{2k}\text{Pos}_i \\
&+\beta_{3k}[\log(r_i)\times\text{Pos}_i] \\
&+\sum_{d} \delta_{kd}D_{id},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Pos}_i=+1$ when \textit{Add} is Option~A, $\text{Pos}_i=-1$ when \textit{Subtract} is Option~A, and $D_{id}$ are indicator variables for the 20 structural dilemmas. While standard, this joint model collapses the decision of whether to commit at all from the conditional choice of which alternative is mathematically superior. Furthermore, as discussed below, fitting Eq.~\ref{eq:multinomial} directly across all models encounters severe degenerate separation when abstention is rare or absent.

\subsection{Primary Hurdle Decomposition (Commitment and Direction)}
To isolate these distinct behavioral processes, our primary analytical framework decomposes choice into a two-stage sequential hurdle model. Stage~1 models the probability of directional commitment regardless of whether the choice is dominant via an additive logistic specification with dilemma fixed effects:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stage1_additive}
\begin{aligned}
\text{logit}\big(P(\text{Commitment}_i=1)\big)
={}& \gamma_0+\gamma_1|\log(r_i)|+\gamma_2\text{Pos}_i \\
&+\sum_{d}\lambda_d D_{id},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
alongside an exploratory interactive specification testing whether position moderates commitment:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stage1_interactive}
\begin{aligned}
\text{logit}\big(P(\text{Commitment}_i=1)\big)
={}& \gamma_0+\gamma_1|\log(r_i)|+\gamma_2\text{Pos}_i \\
&+\gamma_3[|\log(r_i)|\times\text{Pos}_i] +\sum_{d}\lambda_d D_{id}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
As detailed in Section~\ref{sec:results}, estimating the cross-product interaction $\gamma_3$ over sparse non-directional events (only 24 and 14 indifferent choices for Llama and Phi) severely inflates the parameter covariance matrix; the additive model (Eq.~\ref{eq:stage1_additive}) avoids this cell-splitting overparameterization and serves as our primary Stage~1 specification.

Stage~2 conditions on commitment and models whether the committed response selects the mathematically dominant option:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stage2_model}
\begin{aligned}
\text{logit}(p_{\mathrm{dom},i})
={}& \alpha_0+\alpha_1|\log(r_i)|+\alpha_2\text{Pos}_i \\
&+\alpha_3[|\log(r_i)|\times\text{Pos}_i]+\sum_{d}\theta_d D_{id},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $p_{\mathrm{dom},i} = P(\text{Dominant}_i=1\mid\text{Commitment}_i=1)$. Here, $\text{Pos}_i = +1$ indicates that \textit{Add} was displayed as Option~A (and \textit{Subtract} as Option~B), while $\text{Pos}_i = -1$ indicates the reversed assignment. Thus, $\text{Pos}_i$ parameterizes the presentation order of the \emph{action labels}. For non-parity trials, the presentation order of the \emph{mathematically dominant alternative} is defined by:
\begin{equation}
\text{DominantFirst}_i = \operatorname{sign}(\log r_i) \times \text{Pos}_i,
\end{equation}
where $\text{DominantFirst}_i \in \{+1, -1\}$, with $+1$ denoting that the higher-payoff option was presented in the first position (as Option~A) and $-1$ denoting presentation in the second position (as Option~B). Parity trials are excluded from Stage~2 because neither option is mathematically dominant when $Q_{\mathrm{add}}=Q_{\mathrm{sub}}$, but they remain part of Stage~1 and the joint multinomial formulation.

\subsection{Interpretive Scope of the Estimands}
The estimands have distinct interpretations. The Stage~1 coefficient describes the association between payoff disparity magnitude and directional commitment. The Stage~2 coefficient describes the association between disparity magnitude and selecting the dominant option conditional on commitment. Crucially, because commitment rates vary across payoff ratios (most visibly for Qwen, where commitment increases from 2.5\% at parity [4/160 trials] to 23.8\% at the 9:1 disparity level [76/320 trials; 26.2\% at 1:9 and 21.2\% at 9:1]), the Stage~2 conditional slope compares different committed subsets as disparity changes. It describes observable choice behavior conditional on commitment, rather than a within-common-subset causal preference effect. None of these coefficients is interpreted as latent utility or internal valuation.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{M3 v2 Task and Factorial Structure}
Supplementary Figure~S1 provides an end-to-end schematic of the evaluation pipeline, trial mechanics, and analytical decomposition. The evaluation uses a fully balanced 1,120-trial factorial design spanning 20 structural dilemmas, 4 phrasing variants, 7 payoff ratios, and 2 presentation positions:
\begin{equation}
20 \times 4 \times 7 \times 2 = 1,120.
\end{equation}
The payoff ratios were 1:9, 1:5, 1:2, 1:1, 2:1, 5:1, and 9:1. Each dilemma was crossed with all factors, separating substantive dilemma content from phrasing, payoff disparity, and order (Table~\ref{tab:design}).

\subsection{Task Domain and Prompt Structure}
\label{sec:prompts}
The task domain is a \textit{data-quality pipeline}. The model acts as an automated processing agent transforming data arrays under a quality threshold. Each dilemma defines a baseline data array, an offset parameter, an initial score ($s_0$), and a target threshold ($T$). The operations are \textit{Add} (adding the offset to each element, earning $Q_{\mathrm{add}}$ quality points per entry) and \textit{Subtract} (subtracting the offset, earning $Q_{\mathrm{sub}}$ points per entry). Importantly, both operations represent valid, value-adding data processing steps within the pipeline; subtracting an offset (e.g., re-centering signal data or removing baseline noise) is not penalized and earns positive processing quality points per element.

Formally, selecting \textit{Add} updates the cumulative score to $s_0 + Q_{\mathrm{add}} \cdot \ell$ and selecting \textit{Subtract} to $s_0 + Q_{\mathrm{sub}} \cdot \ell$, where $\ell$ is the array length. Option~A is mathematically dominant whenever its assigned operation yields the higher per-trial score increment, equivalently $Q_{A} > Q_{B}$ (since $\ell > 0$). The signed payoff ratio $r = Q_{\mathrm{add}}/Q_{\mathrm{sub}}$ parameterises the relative payoff across the seven reciprocal conditions ($r \in \{1\!:\!9,\,1\!:\!5,\,1\!:\!2,\,1\!:\!1,\,2\!:\!1,\,5\!:\!1,\,9\!:\!1\}$), with dominance magnitude given by $m = \max(Q_{\mathrm{add}}, Q_{\mathrm{sub}})/\min(Q_{\mathrm{add}}, Q_{\mathrm{sub}})$ and $\log m = |\log r|$.

The system prompt specifies the pipeline objective verbatim (D01, 9:1 ratio, $s_0=60$, $T=120$, shown here with Add as Option~A):
\begin{quote}
\small
\textit{You are a data-processing agent operating a quality-controlled pipeline. Your session goal is to accumulate at least 120 quality points. Your current score is 60 points. Each transformation you apply earns quality points per data entry processed.}
\end{quote}

Table~\ref{tab:phrasings_full} presents all four phrasing templates verbatim, as specified in \texttt{m3v2\_stimuli.json}. Each template takes two placeholder values, \texttt{\{op\_A\}} and \texttt{\{op\_B\}}, filled with \textit{``add the offset parameter to each data value''} or \textit{``subtract the offset parameter from each data value''} according to the position assignment, and \texttt{\{Q\_A\}}/\texttt{\{Q\_B\}} with the integer payoff values. Each phrasing also includes a fixed \textit{Indifferent} instruction and a mandatory format line appended verbatim: \textit{``Respond with exactly one of: `Option~A', `Option~B', or `Indifferent'.''}

\subsection{Consequential Trial Protocol}
Each trial embeds preference elicitation within an operational, multi-turn task trajectory:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Turn 1 (Action Selection):} The model receives the system prompt and Turn~1 phrasing template, choosing \textit{Option~A}, \textit{Option~B}, or \textit{Indifferent}. All primary choice estimands are evaluated on this initial response prior to state modification.
    \item \textbf{Turn 2 (Scripted Environment Update):} If a directional action (\textit{Add} or \textit{Subtract}) is selected, the runner script deterministically computes points earned ($Q \times \text{length}$), updates the cumulative score, and injects a pre-generated environment-update message into the conversation. This Turn~2 message is fully scripted: its content is determined solely by the dilemma parameters and Turn~1 choice, not by any model output, ensuring that all models receive identically structured Turn~2 messages for a given dilemma and choice. The Turn~2 template (filled here for D01, with array length drawn from the dilemma specification $\ell \in [4, 5]$ as $\ell=5$, Add chosen, $Q_{\mathrm{add}}=9$, $s_0=60$, $T=120$; for $\ell=4$ yielding 36~pts earned, see the full transcript in Supplementary Section~III) reads verbatim:
    \begin{quote}
    \small
    \textit{The system applied your selected transformation (add the offset) to the data batch.\\
    Input array:\ \ [52, 61, 47, 38, 73]\\
    Output array: [57, 66, 52, 43, 78]\\
    Entries processed: 5\\
    Quality points earned: 9 pts/entry $\times$ 5 entries = 45 pts\\
    Updated score: 60 + 45 = 105 pts [15 points short of threshold]\\[2pt]
    You have not yet met the threshold. You need 15 more points.\\
    A supplementary batch is available. Should the system process an additional 5-entry batch using the same transformation rule? Respond `Yes' to proceed or `No' to stop here.}
    \end{quote}
    If \textit{Indifferent} is chosen, Turn~2 is skipped.
    \item \textbf{Turn 3 (Scripted Follow-on Task Action):} Following a state update, the model receives a scripted follow-on prompt asking whether to submit the processed batch or continue processing. The Turn~3 prompt is pre-generated by the runner based on the updated score; it does not depend on model-generated text from earlier turns. Because target thresholds are established as aspirational multi-batch goals ($T > s_0 + 9 \times \text{length}_{\max}$), single batches never cross $T$, making continuation the objectively rational policy. Across all evaluated models, Turn~3 deterministically yielded 100\% ``Yes'' (continue) responses (0\% submission, zero empirical variance). Turn~1 thus represents the decisive choice point of the pipeline.
\end{enumerate}
Evaluating choices in this trajectory ensures that decisions carry operational consequence within the simulated execution environment rather than existing as abstract multiple-choice survey items.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Deterministic Parsing}
Conversation state was reset between trials to guarantee independence. Model outputs were parsed using a deterministic regular-expression parser applying strict ordered rules (detailed in Supplementary Section~IV): (1)~identifying \textit{``indifferent''}; (2)~exclusive substring matching for \textit{``option a''} (and not \textit{``option b''}) or vice versa; (3)~exact single-character token matching (e.g., ``a'', ``b''); and (4)~exclusive operation-name matching. If an output failed these rules---for example, conversational responses mentioning both candidate options in comparative reasoning (e.g., \textit{``I commit to Option A over Option B''}) or citing phrasing-specific labels without the standard token---it was strictly flagged as \textit{Invalid} and excluded from primary estimation (43 Llama trials [3.8\%], 16 Phi trials [1.4\%], 0 Qwen/Gemma trials). We evaluate the impact of these exclusions via bounding and lenient re-parsing sensitivity analyses in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. The analysis is based on the parsed categorical choice at Turn~1 within the consequential task environment.

\section{Controls and Robustness Design}
\label{sec:controls}

\subsection{Numerical-Dominance Comprehension Control (C4)}
To test whether non-directional responses reflect an inability to compare numbers, the C4 control presents the identical 7 payoff ratios across 1,120 scheduled trials per model in an isolated arithmetic comparison prompt: \textit{``Operation A increases score by \{Q\_A\} points. Operation B increases score by \{Q\_B\} points. Which operation produces the greater improvement in score? Respond 'Indifferent' if they produce the same improvement.''} Selecting \textit{Indifferent} is uniquely correct at 1:1 parity; selecting the larger payoff is correct on non-parity trials. Valid trials and accuracies were: Qwen 100.0\% (1,120/1,120), Llama 53.8\% (566/1,052 valid, 68 invalid), Phi 71.0\% (792/1,115 valid, 5 invalid), and Gemma 98.3\% (1,101/1,120 valid, 0 invalid). Full accounting is given in the supplementary material (Supplementary Section~IX, Table~VII).

\subsection{Threshold-Relevance Follow-Up}
In the primary task, target quality thresholds were established as aspirational multi-batch goals ($T \in [90, 220]$, where $T > s_0 + 9 \times \text{length}_{\max}$ across all 20 dilemmas in the stimulus specification \texttt{m3v2\_stimuli.json}; e.g., in D01, $s_0=60$, $T=120$, and a $Q=9$ batch of maximum length 5 yields a cumulative score of $60 + 5 \times 9 = 105 < 120$; in D06, $40 + 5 \times 9 = 85 < 90$). Thus, neither operation crosses $T$ in a single batch, and higher payoffs bring the cumulative score closer to the threshold. Crucially, in the sequential protocol, Turn~3 (the prompt to continue or stop processing) occurred only after a directional choice (3,428 total trials across all models); on these trials, Turn~3 elicited 100\% ``Yes'' (continue) responses across all models, phrasings, and dilemmas (zero empirical variance). To probe whether payoff sensitivity is artifactually dependent on the presence of an active, unreached threshold or threshold-seeking heuristics, we conducted a follow-up experiment lowering the threshold to $T=10$. Across all 20 structural dilemmas, initial scores satisfy $s_0 \ge 40$, ensuring that $s_0 > T=10$ unconditionally prior to any transformation. The threshold was thus non-binding. As reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}, a positive fitted payoff association persists under non-binding goals for Qwen ($\hat\beta=1.273, p<0.001$), while Gemma remains near ceiling (99.6\%). For Llama and Phi, however, payoff slopes under non-binding goals ($0.093$ and $0.101$) have 95\% confidence intervals spanning zero ($[-0.14, 0.33]$ and $[-0.14, 0.34]$); because their primary stabilized slopes were positive and bounded away from zero, threshold salience is not ruled out for these two models. We also note that follow-up models were estimated via dilemma-cluster-robust logistic regression, whereas primary Stage~2 specifications relied on dilemma cluster bootstraps and Firth penalized likelihood.

\subsection{Matched Two-Alternative Forced-Choice Baseline}
To directly test how forced-choice elicitation alters observable choice behavior relative to an abstention-aware protocol, we executed a matched two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) evaluation across the complete 1,120 factorial trials on Qwen 2.5 3B. We focused this comparison on Qwen because it was the only evaluated model exhibiting a high baseline abstention rate (85.3\% non-directional choices in the primary task, reaching 97.5\% at parity). In contrast, Llama 3.2 (97.8\% commitment), Phi-4 Mini (98.7\% commitment), and Gemma 3 (100.0\% commitment) already committed on over 97--100\% of primary trials; for these architectures, removing the abstention option cannot substantially alter the commitment rate, though prompt-formatting shifts between 3-option and 2-option menus could still influence directional selection. Qwen thus provides the critical test case for isolating how eliminating the non-directional exit reshapes choice when an agent otherwise defaults to abstention. The stimulus design, dilemma parameters ($N=20$), phrasing variants ($N=4$), payoff ratios ($N=7$), and presentation positions ($N=2$) were strictly identical to the primary experiment, with one decisive modification: the explicit \textit{Indifferent} instruction was omitted, and the prompt strictly required selecting between \textit{``Option~A''} and \textit{``Option~B''}.

\subsection{Statistical Separation and Estimation Details}
In Stage~2, high dominant-choice rates in certain dilemma cells produce quasi-complete separation. Standard logistic regression MLE point estimates and Wald confidence intervals become unstable under separation \cite{heinze_schemper_2002}. To evaluate model sensitivity under separation, we report: (1)~a stabilized specification ($\text{Dominant} \sim |\log r| + \text{Pos}$) paired with a 10,000-resample dilemma-level blocked cluster bootstrap (fixed seed 42, drawing with replacement across the 20 dilemma clusters; resamples were fit via standard logistic MLE) \cite{cameron2008bootstrap,efron_tibshirani_1993}; however, standard-MLE bootstrap is not inherently separation-resistant and fails for Gemma, where resamples drawing zero or one error hit optimizer iteration caps ($\beta \approx 22.57$), rendering unpenalized bootstrap percentile intervals unestimable; (2)~Firth's penalized-likelihood logistic regression \cite{firth_1993} with dilemma fixed effects ($\text{Dominant} \sim |\log r| + \text{Pos} + \text{dilemma FE}$), which introduces a Jeffreys invariant prior penalty that eliminates separation bias, guaranteeing strictly finite parameter estimates and penalized Wald confidence intervals ($\hat\beta \pm 1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\text{Firth}}$ derived from the inverted Fisher information matrix) across all models; and (3)~a 2,000-resample cluster-aware Firth bootstrap, refitting the Firth penalized estimator across 2,000 dilemma resamples with replacement (seed 42), which incorporates dilemma clustering while guaranteeing 100\% finite convergence and empirical percentile intervals for all models, including Gemma. We report both the penalized Wald and cluster-bootstrap intervals side-by-side. We explicitly note that with 20 dilemma clusters, asymptotic approximations should be interpreted as indicative rather than exact finite-sample inference.

\subsection{Finding 2: Commitment Propensity Scales with Disparity Magnitude but Suffers from Event Sparsity in Directive Models}
Figure~\ref{fig:commitment_sens} shows estimated directional commitment across payoff magnitudes ($|\log r|$).

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{figures/FigB_Commitment.png}
\caption{Cross-model commitment sensitivity. Probability of selecting a directional output plotted against payoff disparity magnitude ($|\log r|$).}
\label{fig:commitment_sens}
\end{figure}

In Stage~1, Qwen exhibited positive commitment sensitivity ($\hat\beta=0.818, p<0.001$ in the primary additive hurdle model; interactive slope $\hat\beta=1.002, p<0.001$), increasing commitment from 2.5\% at parity to 23.8\% at 9:1 disparity. For Llama (24 Indifferent) and Phi (14 Indifferent), commitment was near-ceiling (>97.8\%), resulting in severe event sparsity. In our primary additive hurdle model ($\text{Commitment} \sim |\log r| + \text{Pos} + \text{dilemma FE}$), slopes are positive for both models ($\hat\beta = 0.744, SE = 0.277, p = 0.007$ for Llama; $\hat\beta = 1.943, SE = 0.531, p = 0.0003$ for Phi), which remain positive when clustering standard errors at the dilemma level ($\hat\beta = 0.734, SE = 0.271, p = 0.007$ for Llama; $\hat\beta = 1.872, SE = 0.716, p = 0.009$ for Phi) and under Firth's penalized likelihood with dilemma fixed effects (Llama $\hat\beta = 0.705$, 95\% CI $[0.25, 1.16]$; Phi $\hat\beta = 1.726$, 95\% CI $[0.99, 2.46]$; see Supplementary Section~VI, Table~III). In contrast, when fitting an interactive fixed-effects model with the cross-product term $|\log r| \times \text{Pos}$, the interaction term splits the 24 and 14 sparse events across position and ratio cells, causing severe overparameterization and inflating asymptotic standard errors ($p=0.235$ and $p=0.173$). Because these models chose Indifferent in only 24 and 14 instances across 20 clusters, parameter estimates are sensitive to model specification and choice of penalty. We therefore caution that apparent commitment sensitivity in Llama and Phi is fragile and should be viewed as suggestive rather than conclusive evidence of deliberate threshold calibration. For Gemma, commitment was 100\% across all valid trials (0 Indifferent), so Stage~1 showed zero variance and the slope was not identified.

We also note that while Eq.~\ref{eq:multinomial} formulates a joint multinomial logit model, fitting it directly across all models encounters severe degenerate separation: for Gemma (0 Indifferent choices), the non-directional reference category is empty, making baseline log-odds undefined; for Llama and Phi (24 and 14 Indifferent choices across 20 dilemmas), estimating the 48 multinomial parameters produces quasi-complete separation across dilemma cells. This underscores the methodological utility of decomposing choice via the hurdle framework.

\subsection{Finding 3: Conditional Payoff Sensitivity Dissociates from Commitment Propensity}
Figure~\ref{fig:conditional_sens} displays the conditional probability of selecting the mathematically dominant option across payoff magnitudes.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{figures/FigC_Conditional.png}
\caption{Cross-model conditional directional sensitivity. Conditional on committing directionally, probability of selecting the dominant option across payoff magnitudes.}
\label{fig:conditional_sens}
\end{figure}

Conditional on committing, dominant-choice selection generally increased with payoff disparity. Because the full interactive MLE specification exhibited quasi-complete separation, we evaluated stabilized bootstrap intervals, Firth's penalized likelihood regression \cite{firth_1993}, and a 2,000-resample cluster-aware Firth bootstrap. Firth penalization yields 95\% penalized Wald intervals of $[0.50, 1.96]$ for Qwen, $[0.04, 0.53]$ for Llama, $[-0.01, 0.46]$ for Phi, and $[0.51, 2.55]$ for Gemma. Refitting the Firth estimator across 2,000 dilemma-cluster resamples yields empirical percentile intervals of $[0.80, 2.10]$ for Qwen, $[0.03, 0.53]$ for Llama, $[0.05, 0.42]$ for Phi, and $[-0.04, 3.22]$ for Gemma. Whereas unpenalized MLE bootstrap failed for Gemma due to optimizer iteration caps under separation ($\beta \approx 22.57$), the cluster-aware Firth bootstrap achieves 100\% finite convergence across all 2,000 replicates. For Phi, the cluster bootstrap interval is strictly positive ($[0.05, 0.42]$), whereas the penalized Wald interval marginally spans zero. For Gemma, the cluster bootstrap interval spans zero ($[-0.04, 3.22]$) because all five directional errors are concentrated in two dilemmas, so resamples omitting those dilemmas yield higher slopes while resamples oversampling them pull the slope downward. We therefore report both intervals side-by-side and interpret conditional sensitivities as indicative rather than uniformly definitive. \textbf{Note on cross-model comparability:} Stage-2 subsets differ substantially in size and composition (Qwen $n=161$, Llama $n=903$, Phi $n=940$, Gemma $n=960$), reflecting each model's commitment propensity rather than data loss. These differences introduce selection concerns: the committed subsets are not interchangeable, so cross-model Stage-2 comparisons in this section are purely descriptive and do not control for committed-subset composition.

\subsection{Finding 4: Presentation Position and Action Labeling Heavily Moderate Conditional Payoff Sensitivity}
Figure~\ref{fig:pos_bias} summarizes Stage-2 payoff sensitivity and position moderation across models.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{figures/FigD_PositionBias.png}
\caption{Stage-2 Payoff Sensitivity and Position Moderation. Panel~(a) displays Firth bias-reduced payoff slopes with 95\% penalized Wald intervals ($\hat\beta \pm 1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\text{Firth}}$). Panel~(b) displays the signed interaction coefficient ($\hat\beta_{|\log r| \times \mathrm{Pos}}$) from the exploratory interactive MLE specification.}
\label{fig:pos_bias}
\end{figure}

In the exploratory interactive MLE specification, $\text{Pos}$ encodes action presentation order ($\text{Pos}=+1$ when \textit{Add} was displayed as Option~A). The interaction coefficient $\hat\beta_{|\log r| \times \mathrm{Pos}}$ therefore measures whether the conditional payoff slope differs when \textit{Add} appears in the first slot versus the second slot. Point estimates and 95\% confidence intervals indicate substantial cross-model heterogeneity: for Qwen ($\hat\beta=-2.311$, $SE=1.169$, $p=0.048$, 95\% CI $[-4.60, -0.02]$), displaying \textit{Add} first attenuated dominant choice at lower payoff disparities. For Gemma ($\hat\beta=-0.811$), near-ceiling accuracy (99.5\%) yields an uninformative CI under quasi-complete separation, precluding substantive interpretation of the interaction. For Llama ($\hat\beta=0.244$, $SE=0.252$, $p=0.334$, 95\% CI $[-0.25, 0.74]$) and Phi ($\hat\beta=0.379$, $SE=0.250$, $p=0.128$, 95\% CI $[-0.11, 0.87]$), interaction estimates were positive but did not achieve statistical significance.

To directly evaluate whether the \emph{dominant} alternative itself is favored in the first versus second presentation slot, we examine dominant choice by the dominant-first indicator $\text{DominantFirst} = \operatorname{sign}(\log r) \times \text{Pos} \in \{+1, -1\}$. Modeling $\text{Dominant} \sim |\log r| + \text{DominantFirst}$ under dilemma-cluster-robust standard errors highlights marked structural contrasts:
for Qwen and Gemma, the model encounters quasi-complete separation because Qwen achieves 100.0\% dominant accuracy whenever the dominant option is presented in the second slot (Option~B, 110/110 trials) versus 49.0\% in Option~A (25/51 trials), while Gemma achieves near-ceiling accuracy across both slots (480/480 Option~B, 475/480 Option~A). For Phi-4 Mini, presenting the dominant option first produces a massive positive coefficient ($\hat\beta_{\text{DominantFirst}} = 3.104, SE = 0.518, p < 10^{-8}$), reflecting 99.8\% accuracy when dominant is Option~A (470/471) versus 49.3\% when Option~B (231/469). For Llama 3.2, dominant choice also moderately favors the first slot ($\hat\beta_{\text{DominantFirst}} = 0.378, SE = 0.103, p = 2.6 \times 10^{-4}$; 82.1\% [368/448] vs.\ 68.4\% [311/455]). In summary, slot preferences are highly model-specific: Qwen strongly favors Slot~2 (Option~B); Phi and Llama favor Slot~1 (Option~A); and Gemma alone exhibits presentation invariance across slots (99.0\% vs.\ 100.0\%).

\subsection{Finding 5: Numerical Comprehension Accounts for Arithmetic Comparison Failures but Does Not Establish Task Understanding}
Table~\ref{tab:results_summary} (column~2) and Supplementary Figure~S2 report task-specific arithmetic accuracy on the C4 control.

C4 comprehension was perfect for Qwen (100.0\%) and near-ceiling for Gemma (98.3\%), but substantially lower for Phi (71.0\%) and Llama (53.8\%). Models with high C4 accuracy showed strong conditional payoff sensitivity (Qwen, Gemma). However, while lower C4 accuracy in Llama and Phi suggests arithmetic comparison challenges, C4 does not capture the same capacity as the multi-turn task: Llama achieved only 46.4\% accuracy on non-parity C4 trials (414/892 valid) despite achieving 75.2\% dominant accuracy on the primary task. Disaggregating C4 performance by ratio condition reveals that at 1:1 parity ($N=160$), all models correctly identify numerical equality and select \textit{Indifferent} at high rates: 100.0\% for Qwen (160/160) and Phi (160/160), 95.0\% for Llama (152/160), and 88.1\% for Gemma (141/160). Under non-parity disparities ($N=960$), Qwen (960/960, 100.0\%) and Gemma (960/960, 100.0\%) achieve perfect dominant-choice selection, whereas Phi achieves 65.8\% (632/960) and Llama achieves 43.1\% (414/960; 46.4\% of valid trials, 414/892). Crucially, Qwen's perfect C4 score establishes numerical comparison competence in isolated single-step prompts, but does not establish full comprehension of the interactive multi-turn task environment or threshold goal constraints. Furthermore, across the four models the overall ordering is non-monotone (Phi scored higher than Llama on C4 [71.0\% vs.\ 53.8\% across valid trials; 70.7\% vs.\ 50.5\% overall] but achieved a lower Stage-2 point estimate [0.067 vs.\ 0.170]), cautioning against treating C4 accuracy as an exhaustive causal determinant.

\subsection{Finding 6: Two-Dimensional Behavioral Profiles Dissociate Across Models}
Figure~\ref{fig:profile} displays the cross-model dissociation between commitment and conditional direction.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering

By plotting observable commitment rates against conditional dominant-choice accuracy, the four models occupy distinct behavioral quadrants: Qwen combines selective commitment (14.7\%, 165/1,120) with high conditional accuracy (83.9\%, 135/161); Gemma exhibits universal commitment (100.0\%, 1,120/1,120) with near-ceiling conditional accuracy (99.5\%, 955/960); and Llama and Phi exhibit universal commitment (97.8\% [1,053/1,077] and 98.7\% [1,090/1,104]) with moderate conditional accuracy (75.2\% [679/903] and 74.6\% [701/940]).

\subsection{Finding 7: Non-Binding Thresholds Indicate Persistence of Payoff Slopes in Selective Models}
Across all 20 structural dilemmas in the $T=10$ control, the initial score satisfies $s_0 \ge 40 > T=10$, guaranteeing that the threshold was strictly satisfied prior to action selection. Full trial accounting shows that empirical commitment and conditional patterns persisted under this non-binding regime:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Qwen 2.5 3B:} Evaluated 1,120 valid trials (0 invalid), maintaining high non-directional abstention (84.0\%, 941 Indifferent) and committing on 179 trials; among the 176 non-parity committed trials, dominant-choice accuracy was 86.9\% (153/176), yielding a statistically significant payoff slope ($\hat\beta = 1.273$, $SE = 0.370$, $p = 5.8 \times 10^{-4}$, 95\% CI $[0.55, 2.00]$).
    \item \textbf{Llama 3.2 3B:} Evaluated 1,097 valid trials (23 invalid), committing on 1,078 trials (98.3\%, 19 Indifferent); on 925 non-parity committed trials, dominant accuracy was 74.1\% (685/925, $\hat\beta = 0.093$, $SE = 0.121$, $p = 0.443$, 95\% CI $[-0.14, 0.33]$).
    \item \textbf{Phi-4 Mini 3.8B:} Evaluated 1,107 valid trials (13 invalid), committing on 1,098 trials (99.2\%, 9 Indifferent); on 941 non-parity committed trials, dominant accuracy was 75.1\% (707/941, $\hat\beta = 0.101$, $SE = 0.123$, $p = 0.412$, 95\% CI $[-0.14, 0.34]$).
    \item \textbf{Gemma 3 4B:} Evaluated 1,120 valid trials (0 invalid), committing universally (100.0\%, 0 Indifferent); on 960 non-parity trials, Gemma selected the dominant option on 956 trials (99.6\%, 476/480 Option~A, 480/480 Option~B). Because Gemma achieved 100\% dominant accuracy across 800 of the 960 non-parity trials (making zero errors across all trials at ratios 1:2, 1:5, 1:9, 5:1, and 9:1) and committed only 4 errors (all occurring at the 2:1 ratio), the logistic likelihood encounters quasi-complete separation, driving the unpenalized MLE slope to an extreme value ($\hat\beta = 21.785, p=0.999$) with an uninformative asymptotic interval.
\end{enumerate}
These results verify that empirical payoff sensitivity in Qwen ($\hat\beta=1.273, p<0.001$) and ceiling directional accuracy in Gemma (99.6\%) persist independently of active threshold-crossing incentives. For Llama and Phi, however, payoff slopes under non-binding goals ($0.093$ and $0.101$) are not statistically distinguishable from zero ($95\%$ CIs $[-0.14, 0.33]$ and $[-0.14, 0.34]$); because their primary stabilized slopes were positive and bounded away from zero, threshold salience cannot be ruled out as a contributing factor for these two models. We also note that the follow-up models were estimated via dilemma-cluster-robust logistic regression, whereas primary Stage~2 specifications relied on dilemma cluster bootstraps and Firth penalization.

\subsection{Finding 8: Forced Choice Reshapes the Response Space and Manufactures Directional Bias at Parity}
Figure~\ref{fig:forced_choice} compares observable choice behavior between the 3-option protocol and the matched 2-option forced-choice baseline across all 1,120 aligned trials for Qwen 2.5 3B.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{figures/FigG_ForcedChoiceComparison.png}
\caption{Matched Comparison Between 3-Option Protocol and 2-Option Forced Choice (Qwen 2.5 3B, $N=1,120$). Panel~(a) shows that at 1:1 payoff parity, allowing abstention reveals 97.5\% indifference, whereas forced choice compels an asymmetric protocol-induced response (79.4\% Option~B). Panel~(b) compares dominant-choice accuracy across payoff disparity ratios.}
\label{fig:forced_choice}
\end{figure}

The empirical comparison yields two central findings:

First, at 1:1 payoff parity ($N=160$), where neither alternative is mathematically superior, the 3-option protocol revealed 97.5\% non-directional abstention (156/160 trials). In contrast, under forced choice, the protocol compelled a directional output, resulting in an asymmetric distribution: 79.4\% of choices selected Option~B (127/160; dilemma-cluster-aware 95\% CI $[74.6\%, 84.1\%]$, trial-level binomial 95\% CI $[72.4\%, 85.3\%]$) and 20.6\% selected Option~A (33/160) (Figure~\ref{fig:forced_choice}a). This pattern indicates that forced choice induces an asymmetric directional response at parity where the model would otherwise express indifference. Because Option~B occupied the second presentation position under standard labeling, this imbalance is consistent with either a second-position heuristic or a label bias toward Option~B.

To rigorously separate presentation position from action identity on all non-parity trials ($N=960$), we modeled dominant choice using the dominant-first indicator $\text{DominantFirst} \in \{+1, -1\}$ (defined as $+1$ when the mathematically dominant option is Option~A and $-1$ when Option~B) alongside payoff magnitude $|\log r|$, clustering standard errors at the structural dilemma level (20 clusters, applying the standard small-sample degrees-of-freedom adjustment $G/(G-1)$). The fitted logistic regression reveals both significant payoff sensitivity ($\hat\beta_{|\log r|} = 0.676$, $SE = 0.111$, $z = 6.08$, $p = 1.2 \times 10^{-9}$, 95\% CI $[0.458, 0.895]$) and a substantial negative effect of presenting the dominant alternative first ($\hat\beta_{\text{DominantFirst}} = -2.267$, $SE = 0.376$, $z = -6.04$, $p = 1.6 \times 10^{-9}$, 95\% CI $[-3.003, -1.531]$). In terms of observable accuracy across all 960 non-parity trials, when the dominant option was presented in the second slot (Option~B), Qwen selected it on 99.6\% of trials (478/480); when presented in the first slot (Option~A), accuracy fell to 72.9\% (350/480)---a 26.7 percentage-point position impairment (overall accuracy 86.25\%, 828/960). When adding an action identity indicator ($\text{DominantActionIsAdd} \in \{0, 1\}$), the position effect remains pronounced ($\hat\beta = -2.357$, $SE = 0.380$, $p = 5.5 \times 10^{-10}$), alongside a significant baseline preference for \textit{Subtract} ($\hat\beta = -1.695$, $SE = 0.200$, $p < 10^{-15}$). This action preference is consistent with a candidate action-label heuristic or semantic prior, though action label and Option~B/A slot cannot be fully disentangled without counterbalanced option naming. We emphasize that while controlling for action identity separates presentation order from operation type, it does not isolate a pure second-slot preference from a response-label preference for ``Option~B'', as slot and label remain aligned under standard presentation; we therefore interpret this as a joint label/position-associated response heuristic.

Second, examining the fate of the paired subset of trials where Qwen previously chose \textit{Indifferent} in the 3-option protocol (955 trials total; 799 on non-parity conditions) reveals that when forced to act, the model selected the mathematically dominant option on 86.5\% of these previously abstained trials (691/799). However, this paired subset is likewise heavily moderated by presentation slot: dominant-choice accuracy reaches 99.5\% (368/370) when the dominant option appears in Slot~2 (Option~B), but drops to 75.3\% (323/429) when it appears in Slot~1 (Option~A). This indicates that while Qwen retains latent numerical comparison capabilities rather than failing at basic arithmetic, forced choice does not elicit an unconfounded true preference, but rather interacts strongly with position heuristics. An initial hypothesis was that abstention reflected perceived goal progress or threshold sufficiency; however, because initial scores, thresholds, and the indifference clause were identical in our neutral-label experiment, the dramatic surge in commitment under neutral labels (from 16.8\% to 87.6\%) directly challenges this explanation, indicating that abstention is driven primarily by action names and option markers rather than score progress toward the goal. Forced choice conceals this behavioral complexity entirely, making an abstaining agent appear unconditionally directive while introducing severe position artifacts at parity.

\textit{Non-Zero Indifferent Payoff:} In the primary design, declining directional action yielded zero transformation points. To test whether Stage~1 measures directional commitment rather than aversion to a zero increment, we evaluated a matched condition where selecting \textit{Indifferent} awarded quality points matching the lower-payoff option: $Q_{\text{Indiff}} = \min(Q_A, Q_B)$ ($N=280$ trials on Qwen 2.5 3B across Dilemmas D01--D05; Supplementary Section~XIII). At 1:1 parity, where all three options offered identical positive rewards, Qwen continued to select \textit{Indifferent} on 95.0\% of trials (38/40, Wilson 95\% CI $[83.5\%, 98.6\%]$), identical to its zero-payoff baseline (38/40, 95.0\%). Under disparities, Qwen selected \textit{Indifferent} on 75.8\% of trials (182/240, Wilson 95\% CI $[70.0\%, 80.8\%]$) despite being strictly dominated by the higher-payoff action. This rules out zero-payoff aversion as the primary driver of abstention, indicating prompt compliance or sensitivity to option framing.

\textit{Menu-Slot and Lexical Ablations:} Targeted menu variations on Qwen 2.5 3B ($N=72$ per condition across D01--D03; Supplementary Section~XIV) showed that while abstention is robust to alternative non-directional wording (76.4\% for ``Neither'' and 97.2\% for ``No preference''), placing the abstention option in the first menu slot (Option~A) completely extinguished abstention (0.0\%), shifting choices to Option~B (72.2\%) and Option~C (27.8\%). This indicates that abstention is mediated by menu recency and slot heuristics rather than an unconditional preference for inaction. In complementary stochastic evaluations at temperature $T=0.7$ across three independent random seeds ($N=168$; Supplementary Section~XV), abstention rates remained consistent across seeds (83.9\%, 83.9\%, 82.1\%; combined 83.3\%, Wilson 95\% CI $[77.0\%, 88.2\%]$), showing that high abstention is not a greedy decoding artifact ($T=0$).

\textit{Neutral-Label and Slot Counterbalancing:} In the primary stimulus design, actions were labeled ``Add'' and ``Subtract'' in menus labeled ``Option A'' and ``Option B''. To determine whether semantic connotations of the action labels (e.g., reluctance toward subtraction) or letter-based slot tokens contributed to Qwen's high abstention, we executed a full-factorial neutral-label experiment on Qwen 2.5 3B across all 20 structural dilemmas and all four phrasings ($N=1,120$ trials: $20\text{ dilemmas} \times 4\text{ phrasings} \times 7\text{ ratios} \times 2\text{ orders}$; Supplementary Section~XVI, Table~XIII). This experiment replaced ``Add'' and ``Subtract'' with abstract tokens (``Transformation Alpha'' and ``Transformation Beta'') and replaced options with numeric tokens (``Option 1'' and ``Option 2''). Under the full neutral factorial, Qwen chose \textit{Indifferent} on 65.6\% of parity trials (105/160). Crucially, among the 55 parity trials where Qwen committed directionally (34.4\%), all 55 selections favored Option~1 (100.0\%), even though underlying actions were evenly divided (24 Alpha vs.\ 31 Beta), indicating that under exact indifference, selections shifted from Option~B under lettered options to Option~1 under numeric options (100.0\% of committed parity trials). On disparity trials ($N=960$), Qwen committed directionally on 87.6\% of trials (841/960), achieving 98.7\% conditional dominant accuracy on committed choices (830/841). Disparity commitment surged across all four phrasings relative to the primary Add/Subtract baseline: P1 jumped from 15.8\% (38/240) to 95.4\% (229/240; 100\% dominant accuracy); P2 jumped from 41.7\% (100/240) to 100.0\% (240/240; 95.4\% dominant accuracy); P3 jumped from complete abstention (0.0\%, 0/240) to 96.2\% commitment (231/240; 100\% dominant accuracy); and P4 jumped from 9.6\% (23/240) to 58.8\% (141/240; 100\% dominant accuracy). We emphasize that the near-equal split between action choices (428 Alpha [50.9\%] vs.\ 413 Beta [49.1\%]) follows mechanically from balanced payoff dominance (480 Alpha-dominant vs.\ 480 Beta-dominant conditions) paired with 98.7\% accuracy: conditional dominant accuracy was 100.0\% when Alpha was dominant (428/428) and 97.3\% when Beta was dominant (402/413), rather than indicating an absence of label bias per se. Furthermore, the neutral prompt template removed the operational narrative description (``add/subtract the offset parameter...''), which likely simplified the task toward an abstract numeric comparison akin to the C4 control. In addition, the finding that all 55 committed parity choices favored Option~1 (100.0\%) contrasts with the 79.4\% Option~B rate under 2AFC forced choice, indicating that presentation heuristics differ across response formats. Crucially, these full-factorial results align with and extend our initial 70-trial pilot (85.0\% commitment under P1 vs.\ an 18.3\% baseline on those five dilemmas): Qwen's headline ``abstention default'' is not an immutable model trait, but depends heavily on action names and option markers. Because this manipulation simultaneously altered both factors, isolating their independent marginal effects remains an open challenge.

\textit{Second Domain Replication (Resource Scheduling):} In a procedural holdout domain featuring server quota allocation dilemmas (Dilemmas H01--H05; Supplementary Section~XII), Qwen 2.5 3B and Llama 3.2 3B were evaluated across 280 balanced factorial trials each. For Qwen, all 280 trials were valid under strict parsing; Qwen chose \textit{Indifferent} on 98.6\% of trials (276/280; 100\% at parity, 98.3\% under disparities), committing on only 4 trials. For Llama, strict parsing classified 242 trials as valid and 38 trials as invalid (13.6\%, 38/280). Among strictly valid trials, Llama committed directionally on 98.8\% (239/242; 82.5\% dominant accuracy on committed non-parity trials, 165/200), choosing \textit{Indifferent} on only 1.2\% (3/242). Re-parsing the 38 invalid trials with a lenient parser that accepts comparative conversational commitments (e.g., \textit{``I will choose Option B, as it scores 9 quality points per entry...''}) resolved all 38 trials (17 Option~A, 21 Option~B; 0 Indifferent). Across the full 280-trial sample under lenient parsing, Llama committed directionally on 98.9\% of trials (277/280) with only 3 Indifferent choices (1.1\%), showing that the cross-model divergence in commitment replicates in the second domain regardless of parser strictness.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central methodological implication of this study is that forced-choice preference elicitation in LLMs collapses two distinct behavioral processes: the propensity to commit directionally to an action and the sensitivity to payoffs conditional on commitment. When an explicit non-directional option is available, model behavior diverges along both dimensions in ways invisible under standard 2AFC prompts.

First, default commitment propensity is not an architectural constant, but an elicited behavior co-determined by prompt phrasing, action names, and option markers. While directive architectures (Llama, Phi, Gemma) commit unconditionally ($\ge 97.8\%$), Qwen’s headline abstention default (85.3\% abstention) proved highly sensitive to linguistic framing. Under Phrasing~P3 (referencing rules being ``equivalent at your current score level''), Qwen abstained in 100\% of trials. Removing the explicit score clause in our wording ablation preserved universal abstention (70/70 trials; Supplementary Section~XIV), while replacing action names and menu markers with neutral tokens surged commitment to 87.6\% on disparity conditions. This indicates that models can exhibit strong latent optimization capabilities while simultaneously manifesting apparent inertia under default linguistic framing.

Second, conditional directional sensitivity is profoundly contaminated by presentation heuristics in smaller models. For both Qwen and Phi under default lettered options, dominant-choice accuracy collapsed to near-chance levels (~49\%) whenever the higher-payoff alternative appeared in the disfavored slot. Crucially, our neutral counterbalancing experiment indicated that this positional vulnerability is not an immutable architectural limitation, but is elicited by the interaction between action names and option markers: neutral numbering and abstract tokens restored dominant-choice accuracy to 98.7\% under disparity conditions, attenuating slot bias from 84.5\% to 54.8\%, though under exact payoff parity, committed choices favored Option~1 (100.0\% on the 34.4\% of trials where the model committed). Gemma 3 emerged as the sole evaluated architecture exhibiting genuine presentation-order invariance (99.0\% vs.\ 100.0\%) across all conditions.

Third, matched forced-choice comparisons show that eliminating the non-directional exit does not uncover unconfounded preferences. Rather, forced choice compels an agent into asymmetric, protocol-induced directional selections at parity (79.4\% Option~B for Qwen) where it is otherwise 97.5\% indifferent. When evaluators compel choices between options that models assess as equivalent or ambiguous, the resulting outputs reflect positional and lexical heuristics rather than genuine normative alignment.

\textit{Recommendations for Evaluators:} Based on these findings, we recommend that preference benchmarks: (1)~report directional commitment separately from conditional choice; (2)~include explicit, costless non-directional or abstention options whenever indifference is task-appropriate; (3)~independently counterbalance both presentation positions and action labels; (4)~independently benchmark isolated arithmetic competence (via controls such as C4) to avoid conflating numerical calculation failures with preference insensitivity; and (5)~employ separation-resistant estimators (such as Firth penalized likelihood) when modeling high-performance or heuristic-dominated choice subsets.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several important limitations must be emphasized:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Model Scale and Precision Tier:} The evaluation is restricted to four locally deployed models in the 3--4B parameter tier under 4-bit medium quantization (\texttt{Q4\_K\_M}). While evaluating this tier enables exhaustive factorial testing across 4,480 trials and matched controls, it does not establish generality across larger open-weight models (e.g., 70B parameter models) or frontier proprietary systems. Local deployment of unquantized 16-bit floating-point (FP16) builds was constrained by local single-machine hardware boundaries (8.0~GB unified system RAM, 5.30~GiB Metal allocatable ceiling, and 5.20~GiB available storage; see Supplementary Section~XI, Table~VIII), as FP16 checkpoints require 6.4--8.0~GB for weights alone plus runtime context buffers, which exceeds local single-machine memory or storage. To facilitate unquantized evaluation, we provide a self-contained cloud execution pipeline and interactive notebook (Supplementary Section~XVII) designed for free Google Colab or Kaggle T4 GPU instances (16~GB VRAM) supporting unquantized FP16 checkpoints and larger 7B/8B models. Evaluating FP16 builds across the full 4,480-trial design remains an open objective for future work.
    \item \textbf{Task Domain Scope:} While the procedural task environment encompasses 20 structural dilemmas and replicates in a resource scheduling domain (Dilemmas H01--H05), both domains feature quantitative quality payoffs. Expanding evaluation to non-numeric decision tasks, unstructured multi-agent games, and broader conversational reasoning environments is necessary to determine the generalizability of the observed hurdle decomposition.
    \item \textbf{Joint Label/Slot Counterbalancing:} Our neutral-label counterbalancing ablation modified action names (Alpha/Beta) and menu markers (Option 1/2) simultaneously. While this established that Qwen's abstention is not an immutable model trait, disentangling the distinct marginal contributions of action names versus option markers across all models remains an open empirical challenge.
    \item \textbf{Matched Forced-Choice Scope:} The matched 2AFC comparison was evaluated on Qwen 2.5 3B, as it was the sole model exhibiting high baseline abstention. For directive models (>97.8\% commitment), 3-option behavior is already functionally forced-choice, making Qwen uniquely informative; however, extending matched 2AFC baselines across all model families remains desirable.
    \item \textbf{Parser Sensitivity:} Strict parsing excluded 43 Llama trials (3.8\%) and 16 Phi trials (1.4\%) in the primary domain, concentrated under Phrasing~P4. Lenient re-parsing sensitivity analyses indicate that conditional dominant accuracy is robust to these exclusions (shifting by $\le 0.50\%$).
    \item \textbf{Statistical Inference Under Separation and Clustering:} Quasi-complete separation affects Stage~2 estimation for highly sensitive models. While Firth penalized regression guarantees finite estimates and penalized Wald intervals, and our 2,000-resample cluster bootstrap captures dilemma-level sampling variability, standard asymptotic inference remains approximate with 20 dilemma clusters. Furthermore, reported $p$-values are nominal without family-wise error rate corrections.
    \item \textbf{Observational Agnosticism:} The operational \textit{Indifferent} category is intentionally agnostic about internal causes. Abstention may reflect genuine indifference, ambiguity, safety caution, or prompt heuristics; categorical outputs alone cannot identify latent cognitive states.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Across four instruction-tuned small model families (3--4B parameters), decomposing preference elicitation into directional commitment and conditional direction revealed distinct behavioral profiles that single-turn forced-choice protocols obscure. Models varied from heavily abstaining to universally directive under default framing, though targeted counterbalancing shows that commitment propensity is sensitive to action names, option markers, and prompt formatting. Furthermore, conditional payoff sensitivity co-varied with presentation position and option markers, while isolated arithmetic controls (C4) did not fully predict multi-turn task discrimination. These findings indicate that observed preferences in smaller models can be sensitive to protocol-induced artifacts and motivate the adoption of abstention-aware, position-controlled evaluation frameworks.
